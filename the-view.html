The View

The corner resolves

Nobody was wrong. They were measuring from different stakes.

Twenty thinkers walked at the same wall from twenty directions, and the last of them showed you that the wall is partly of our own making — that science bought its power by agreeing not to look at the one thing you are surest of. So here is what is left. Not an answer to the hard problem; this book cannot hand you one and will not pretend to. Something smaller and, I think, more useful: one of the field’s oldest fights turns out to be an artifact. Not a hard truth about the world. A consequence of where three careful people each decided to stop.

The fight

It runs through the whole book. Is consciousness built up, or broken down?

One camp says built up. Start with something tiny that has the faintest glimmer of experience, couple enough of them together, and a mind assembles from the bottom. The other camp says broken down. Start with one vast field of experience — everything, all at once — and let it split. Each of us is a pocket that got walled off from the rest.

Up or down. Many becoming one, or one becoming many. Every serious thinker in this book stands somewhere on that line, and the two ends have been shouting past each other for a century.

Both ends need a place to stand.

Four stakes in the ground

Watch what each side actually does when it gets specific.

Jonathan Schooler and Justin Riddle build minds out of nested windows — small fast ones inside bigger slower ones, like rooms inside rooms, each with its own tempo. It is careful, testable neuroscience. And at the top they put an apex: one final window where it all comes together into the single you that is reading this.

Then they say this, in their own paper:

“One could utilize the NOW Model without the inclusion of an apex observer window… However, it is our opinion that the unitary subjective vantage point necessitates its inclusion.” — Riddle & Schooler, Neuroscience of Consciousness, 2024 [verbatim]

Read that twice. The model runs fine without the top. They add it because if they don’t, the unified you leaks out of the theory — and they flag it, honestly, as an opinion.

Now the other end. Bernardo Kastrup starts from one universal consciousness and asks where it splits. To answer, he first has to argue that boundaries in nature are not real things but conveniences we draw. His example is a car. Are the spark plugs part of it? Then what about the fuel, the air that cools the engine, the road under the tyres, the gravity holding it down? His conclusion:

“The decision of where to stop is motivated by convenience.” — Kastrup, Analytic Idealism, 2019, §3.11 [verbatim]

And then he stops. At living creatures with unified experience — you, me, a dog. His own argument says every stopping point is arbitrary, and he needs outside help to justify the one he picks.

Third stake. The same two authors, writing about time, need a fixed clock to measure felt time against — so they keep the block universe’s time as “the universal reference frame upon which all clocks are calibrated.” Immediately after, in brackets:

“(although we are aware of the added complication when considering warped space-time in general relativity)” — Schooler & Riddle, Possibility Studies & Society, 2024 [verbatim]

There is no universal reference frame in relativity. Einstein removed it. They know, and they set it in a parenthesis and move on.

And the fourth is underneath all of it.

Push the built-up camp on its hardest question — how do tiny flickers of experience add up to this, the particular warmth of one afternoon? — and Hunt and Schooler answer in a supplementary appendix most readers never open. Their answer is elegant: resonance. Things that vibrate together at the same rate stop being a crowd and start being one thing. Macro-qualities are the sum of micro-qualities, the way a chord is the sum of its notes.

It works. But look at what it stands on:

“All actual entities include, by definition, the qualities of consciousness.” — Hunt & Schooler, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2019, Supplementary Appendix 2 [verbatim]

By definition. The experience at the bottom is not derived, discovered, or argued for. It is placed there so the addition has something to add. Asked whether those base qualities are even rich enough to build a mind out of, they answer, in a bracket: “which we don’t take a position on.”

Four theories. Four absolutes — a top, a stopping point, a fixed clock, and a floor. Not one of them falls out of its own theory. Each is driven in by hand at precisely the spot where something would otherwise run out, and — to their real credit — each author says so in print.

What the fight was made of

Here is the thing about a fight over which end is prior: it needs ends.

Take the stakes away and look at what is left. A structure with no highest level and no lowest one. Not endless — closed. Something that comes back around.

On a shape like that, “up” and “down” are perfectly good directions where you are standing, and mean nothing globally. Which is exactly the situation. Combination and dissociation stop being rival accounts of how minds come to be, and become two directions of travel on one surface. Neither prior. Not a compromise between the camps — a dissolution of the thing they were fighting over.

Each side can see the other’s stake

There is a lovely detail here, and it is the reason I do not think either camp was being careless.

Kastrup’s sharpest strike at the built-up camp is an image. They look at the world, find that it is made of tiny particles, and conclude that mind must be built of tiny minds. His reply: you are reading the pattern of the ripples back into the water.

“Individual ripples make up the structure of the movements of water, not of water itself… Our unitary subjectivity is not necessarily composed of myriad subatomic-level subjects for the same reason that water is not made of ripples.” — Kastrup, Philosophies 2(2):10, 2017, §8 [verbatim]

That is not a stake. That is an argument, and a good one. Notice what it attacks: the floor — the very absolute the other camp drove in by definition one section ago.

Then, two paragraphs later, writing about his own ground:

“Because experiences are excitations of TWE, the latter — by definition — cannot be experienced in its unexcited state.” — Kastrup, Philosophies 2(2):10, 2017, §8 [verbatim]

The same move. And to his credit he immediately says the obvious thing against himself — that water without ripples can be seen, while an unexcited ground never can, and so it “may not exist.”

So each camp has its opponent’s hidden assumption in perfect focus, and cannot see the one under its own feet. Nobody here is sloppy. This is what it looks like from inside a real problem: the thing you had to assume in order to start is the thing you can no longer see.

The fight was never about the evidence. It was about four stakes, and every one of them is admitted — most of them by the person who drove it in.

But time still flows

An honest objection, and it nearly sinks this.

Fine — no top, no bottom. But time goes somewhere. You are older than when you started reading. Coffee cools; it never uncools. If there is a direction to that, isn’t there a direction to everything, and doesn’t that restore the arrow the whole argument just removed?

No — and the reason is in Schooler and Riddle’s time paper.

They make felt time a ratio. Not a substance that flows, but a rate — how fast your window processes, measured against something else’s rate. Big slow structures live in slow time; small fast ones in fast time. That is why you cannot swat a fly. The fly is not quicker than you. The fly is watching you move in slow motion.

Sit with the arithmetic of that. A ratio needs two rates. It does not need an absolute.

Which means the fixed clock they drove into the ground — the one relativity forbids — was never load-bearing. Their own machinery only ever compares nested windows to each other. Pull the universal frame out and the flow survives untouched. What you lose is the single cosmic arrow. What you keep is real, definite, measurable direction everywhere you happen to be standing.

Their own model shows it. In the NOW model the direction of flow reverses depending on what you are doing: information runs fast-to-slow when you perceive, and slow-to-fast when you decide to act. Same brain, same windows, opposite flow. Direction set by the process, not by the structure.

Two trains in a station, side by side. One begins to move. For a moment you genuinely cannot tell which. The motion is completely real — and there is no fact about which one is really moving. That is not a failure of your senses. It is what motion is.

The shape, held loosely

If you want a picture, take a Klein bottle: a closed surface with no edge and no consistent outside. Not a claim that the universe is one. A shape whose likeness constrains — the same way the holographic principle gets used long before anyone can write down the real thing.

Two properties do the work. It is closed — no terminals to fight over. And it is non-orientable — no way to define “up” that holds everywhere, though every local direction is perfectly definite.

There is a third feature, and it is free. A Klein bottle cannot fit into three dimensions without appearing to pass through itself. Look at a model of one and you will see a wall where the surface seems to cross. There is no wall. The intersection is an artifact of looking at a four-dimensional object from inside three.

Which is the whole book’s suspicion about boundaries, in one object you can hold.

What this does not settle

It does not touch the hard problem. Why there is something it is like to taste the lemon remains exactly as unanswered as when you started, and anyone who tells you a topology solved it is selling something.

Nor is the alternative built. Saying a closed structure supplies what a top supplied is a claim about what could work, not a demonstration that it does. Three theories with an admitted patch, against one alternative that targets the patch and hasn’t been constructed — that is a better position, not a victory.

And the ground is testable, which is the part that matters. Schooler’s apex predicts a specific slow rhythm in the brain, somewhere near a fifth of a cycle per second, possibly living in the thin top layer of the cortex. Go and look. If unity survives where that rhythm is broken, the apex was scaffolding — and the corner gets narrower again.

That is the honest end of it. The walls are real, and one of them turns out to have been three stakes and a shared assumption. The corner is smaller tonight than it was.

We keep painting.
