The View

The corner resolves

Ask every one of them the same question, and something gives way.

You have met twenty-one minds now, and they do not agree. Not on details — on everything. Consciousness is fundamental; consciousness is a trick of the brain. It is everywhere; it is only here. You would be forgiven for closing the book with the suspicion that this is not a field at all, just a long corridor of brilliant people talking past each other. That suspicion is worth taking seriously, and this last page takes it seriously. It will not hand you an answer to the hard problem. It will do something narrower: take the single deepest disagreement in the whole book and show you that a large part of it is not a disagreement.

The tallest mountain in the world

Everest. You knew that one before you could read.

Now here is a geographer who says Mauna Kea, in Hawaii. She is not being difficult. Everest rises about five and a half miles above sea level, but Mauna Kea starts on the floor of the Pacific, and from its own base to its own summit it stands more than four thousand feet taller than Everest. Most of it is simply underwater.

And here is a third, who says Chimborazo, in Ecuador — a mountain most people have never heard of. The Earth is not a sphere; it bulges at the equator. Chimborazo sits almost on that bulge, so its summit is the farthest point on the entire surface of the planet from the Earth’s centre. Stand on top of it and nothing on Earth is further out into space than you are.

Three mountains. Three answers. Nobody has made a mistake, and no better instrument will settle it, because they are not disagreeing about the mountains at all. They are disagreeing about where to put the zero.

And notice — this is the part that matters — none of them could have said anything without picking one. You cannot measure a height without first deciding what counts as the bottom. The choice is not sloppiness. It is the price of being able to speak at all.

Sea level is the usual pick, and it feels like the neutral one. It isn’t. There is no surface out there called sea level; it is an average, agreed on by people, of a sea that is never still.

Hold on to that question — where did you put the zero? — because we are about to ask it of some very serious people.

The oldest fight in the book

Here is the disagreement that runs under all the others.

Is a mind built up, or broken down?

One camp says built up. Begin with something tiny — an atom, a particle — and grant it the faintest flicker of experience. Almost nothing. Then couple enough of those flickers together, tightly enough, and a mind assembles from underneath. You are a very large number of very small somethings, cooperating.

The other camp says broken down. Begin with one enormous field of experience — all of it, undivided. Then let it split. You are not an assembly; you are a pocket that got walled off. A whirlpool is not made of anything the river isn’t.

Up or down. Many becoming one, or one becoming many. Almost every thinker in this book stands somewhere on that line, and the two ends have been shouting across it for a century without moving.

Now ask them where they put the zero.

Where did you put the zero?

Start with the built-up camp, in its most careful modern form. Jonathan Schooler and Justin Riddle describe the mind as nested windows — small quick ones inside larger slower ones, rooms inside rooms, each with its own tempo. It is real, testable neuroscience with real predictions. And at the very top they place an apex: one final window where everything gathers into the single you reading this sentence.

Where did that top come from? They tell you, in their own paper:

“One could utilize the NOW Model without the inclusion of an apex observer window… However, it is our opinion that the unitary subjective vantage point necessitates its inclusion.” — Riddle & Schooler, Neuroscience of Consciousness, 2024 [verbatim]

The theory runs without it. They put it there because if they don’t, the unified you drains out of the model — and they say so, in the open, and call it an opinion.

That is a stake in the ground. Now the other camp.

Bernardo Kastrup begins with one universal consciousness and has to explain where it divides. To get there he first argues that boundaries in nature are not found but drawn. Take a car. Are the spark plugs part of it? Then why not the fuel, the air cooling the engine, the road under the tyres, the gravity holding it down? Where the car ends is a decision, not a discovery:

“The decision of where to stop is motivated by convenience.” — Kastrup, Analytic Idealism, 2019, §3.11 [verbatim]

And then he stops. At living creatures with unified experience — you, me, a dog. His own argument has just established that every stopping point is a convenience, and he needs help from outside the argument to justify the one he picks.

Two camps. Two stakes. By now you can probably guess what we find if we keep asking.

We do keep asking, and we do find it. Pressed on why felt time flows when physics says it doesn’t, the same two authors need something fixed to measure felt time against — so they quietly keep the old universal clock, “the universal reference frame upon which all clocks are calibrated,” and then add, in a bracket:

“(although we are aware of the added complication when considering warped space-time in general relativity)” — Schooler & Riddle, Possibility Studies & Society, 2024 [verbatim]

There is no universal reference frame. Einstein removed it a century ago. They know; the knowledge goes in a parenthesis.

And pressed on the hardest question of all — how do tiny flickers add up to this, the particular warmth of one afternoon — the built-up camp answers with resonance. Things vibrating at the same rate stop being a crowd and become one thing, the way notes become a chord. It is a good answer. It rests on this:

“All actual entities include, by definition, the qualities of consciousness.” — Hunt & Schooler, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2019, Supplementary Appendix 2 [verbatim]

By definition. The experience at the bottom is not found, derived, or argued for. It is placed there, so that the adding-up has something to add.

Four theories. Four places where a careful thinker reached the edge of what the argument could carry, drove something into the ground, and measured from it. A top. A stopping point. A fixed clock. A floor. Not one of the four falls out of its own theory — and to their lasting credit, every one of them says so in print.

The part that nearly breaks this

If the story ended there it would be too neat, and it doesn’t end there.

Kastrup has a genuine argument against the other camp, and it is not a stake. They look at the world, find it made of tiny particles, and conclude that mind must likewise be made of tiny minds. His reply is an image:

“Individual ripples make up the structure of the movements of water, not of water itself… Our unitary subjectivity is not necessarily composed of myriad subatomic-level subjects for the same reason that water is not made of ripples.” — Kastrup, Philosophies 2(2):10, 2017, §8 [verbatim]

That is a real objection, and a good one. You cannot read the pattern of the ripples back into the water. So no — the fight is not purely a misunderstanding. There is something being argued about.

But look at what the objection attacks. It attacks the floor. It goes straight for the stake the other camp drove in by definition, and it does not miss.

And then, two paragraphs later, writing about his own ground:

“Because experiences are excitations of TWE, the latter — by definition — cannot be experienced in its unexcited state.” — Kastrup, Philosophies 2(2):10, 2017, §8 [verbatim]

The identical move. To his credit he immediately raises the obvious objection against himself — that water without ripples can be seen, while an unexcited ground never can, and so, he admits, it “may not exist.”

There it is. Each camp has the other’s hidden assumption in perfect focus and cannot see the one under its own feet. Nobody here is careless. This is what a hard problem does to careful people: the thing you had to assume in order to begin is the thing you can no longer see.

Pull the stakes

So take them out. All four. What is left?

A structure with no highest level and no lowest one. Not endless, which would be its own kind of nonsense — closed. Something that comes back around.

On a shape like that, “up” and “down” are perfectly good directions from wherever you happen to stand, and mean nothing at all globally. Which is exactly our situation. Built-up and broken-down stop being rival accounts of how a mind comes to be and become two directions of travel on one surface. Neither first. Not a truce between the camps — the quiet removal of the thing they were fighting over.

“But time still goes somewhere”

The strongest objection to all of this, and it deserves a straight answer.

Fine, no top and no bottom. But time has a direction. You are older than when you started reading. Coffee cools and never uncools. If that asymmetry is real, hasn’t the arrow come back?

No — and the reason is sitting in the same paper that drove in the fixed clock.

They make felt time a ratio. Not a substance that flows, but a rate: how fast your window turns, measured against how fast something else turns. Large slow structures live in slow time; small quick ones in quick time. It is why you cannot swat a fly. The fly is not faster than you. The fly is watching you move through treacle.

Now notice the arithmetic. A ratio needs two rates. It does not need an absolute.

Which means the universal clock — the one relativity forbids, the one they parked in a bracket — was never carrying the weight. Their own machinery only ever compares one window to another. Pull the clock out and the flow survives completely intact. What you lose is a single cosmic arrow. What you keep is real, definite direction everywhere you actually are.

Their own model already shows it. In the nested-window picture the direction of flow reverses depending on what you are doing: information runs fast-to-slow while you perceive, and slow-to-fast when you decide to move. Same brain, same windows, opposite direction. Set by the process, not by the universe.

Two trains at a platform, side by side. One begins to move, and for a second you honestly cannot tell which. The motion is entirely real. There is no fact about which one is really moving. That is not your eyes failing. That is what motion is.

A shape to hold it in

If you want a picture: a Klein bottle. A closed surface with no edge and no consistent outside.

Not a claim that the universe is one. A shape whose likeness constrains — used the way physicists used the holographic principle for years before anyone could write the real thing down. Two of its properties do the work. It is closed, so there are no ends to fight over. And it is non-orientable, so there is no way to define “up” that holds everywhere, while every local direction stays perfectly definite.

There is a third feature, and it comes free. A Klein bottle cannot fit into three dimensions without appearing to pass through itself. Look at any model of one and you will see a wall where the surface seems to cross. There is no wall there. The intersection is an artifact of viewing a four-dimensional object from inside three.

Which is this book’s whole suspicion about boundaries, in an object you can hold in one hand.

What you can take with you

Not an answer to the hard problem. Why there is something it is like to taste a lemon is exactly as open as it was on the first page, and anyone who tells you a topology solved it is selling something.

Nor is the alternative built. Saying that a closed structure supplies what a top supplied is a claim about what might work, not a demonstration that it does. Four theories with an admitted stake, against one alternative that aims at the stakes and hasn’t yet been constructed — that is a better position, not a victory.

And it is testable, which is the part that matters. The apex predicts a specific slow rhythm in the brain, somewhere near a fifth of a cycle per second, perhaps in the thin top layer of the cortex. Go and look. If the unified you survives where that rhythm is broken, the top was scaffolding, and the corner gets narrower again.

But the question is yours now, and it is portable. When you next find two careful people in a disagreement that will not resolve — in physics, in medicine, in an argument at your own table — you have somewhere to stand. Before asking which of them is right, ask each one where they put the zero.

Sometimes there is no argument. Sometimes it is only the sea, averaged, and agreed on by people.
