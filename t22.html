The Screen · Twenty-two

Markus Müller

Stop asking what the world is. Ask what you should expect to see next.

Every other living thinker in this book is trying, in one way or another, to explain consciousness. Markus Müller is not. He is a mathematical physicist in Vienna, he works on the foundations of quantum theory, and he will tell you — in a footnote, with some heat — that consciousness plays no fundamental role in anything he does. He belongs in this book anyway, and for an unusual reason: starting from a question that has nothing to do with mind, using nothing but information theory, he arrived at the same strange place as the mystics, and then refused to draw the mystical conclusion. He is the best evidence this book has that the shape it keeps finding is not wishful thinking.

The first second of waking up

You surface somewhere dark and, for a moment, you do not know where you are.

Not fear exactly — just a gap. A hotel room, maybe. Your old bedroom. There is a self here, thinking, but it has not yet been handed a world. Then it lands: this room, this Tuesday, this life, and the gap closes so fast you almost never catch it.

Müller builds physics out of that gap.

Notice what you actually had in the dark. Not a world. A pattern — whatever you are, right then, at that instant: memories, half-thoughts, the shape of the pillow against your face. And notice what happened next was a prediction that came true. You expected to be you, here, continuing. You were right. You are right every morning, and it is so reliable you have never once counted it as evidence of anything.

Müller thinks it is evidence, and that we have been reading the question backwards.

The question everybody skips

Physics asks: what is the case in the world? Build the world, put yourself in it, and what you see follows.

Müller asks the other one: what should I expect to experience next?

That sounds like the same question wearing different clothes. It is not, and here is how he proves it. There are situations — not fantasies, but setups you can write down in ordinary quantum theory — where physics provably cannot tell each observer what they should expect to see. Not “we haven’t worked it out yet.” There is no consistent joint answer to be had. Physics, asked the first-person question, sometimes returns silence.

Most physicists shrug at this and say the question was badly posed. Müller refuses. If a person is going to have an experience, then what they should expect is a real question with a real answer, and a theory that goes quiet there has a hole in it.

So he sets out to fill the hole from the other end.

A world that builds itself

Here is the part that earns him a page.

Start with almost nothing. No space, no time, no matter. Just an observer’s information — a pattern — and one rule about what comes next: simpler continuations are more likely than complicated ones. That rule is not a guess; it is the backbone of algorithmic information theory, the mathematics of how much you have to say to specify a thing.

Give it that, and let it run.

What emerges, provably, inside a small toy model, is a stable, lawful, shared world. Not chaos. Not a private dream. A world with regularities you can rely on, and — this is the part that should stop you — a world that other patterns compute their way into as well. Ask whether anyone else is really there, and the mathematics answers yes: another sufficiently complex pattern infers the same world you do. Somebody is genuinely at home.

He did not set out to make the universe depend on observers. He set out to fix a hole in quantum theory, and a room assembled itself around the point of view.

There is no you that lasts

One more move, and it is the strangest.

If the primitive is the pattern, then the primitive is momentary. Müller calls these self states — you at one instant, the whole of you, nothing carried over. Not a person: a person-stage. Continuing selves are not fundamental in his theory; they are not even in it.

Which means the you of this morning does not travel to tonight. Something happens that is much odder and much more ordinary: a pattern very much like this one finds itself here, with these memories, and calls the whole run “me.”

The nugget

The world may not be a place you are standing in. It may be the answer to a question you are constantly asking without noticing: what comes next? Ask it from nothing but a pattern and a preference for simplicity, and a lawful, shared world assembles itself around the asking — with other people really in it. And the asker is not a soul that endures. It is whatever you are, right now, at this instant, being asked again.

Hold it honestly

This page needs a warning label the others don’t, and it points in the opposite direction to the one you’d expect.

The trap is the word “idealism.” It is in the title of his own programme, and it will lead you somewhere he refuses to go. Müller’s idealism is first-person-perspective primacy — the view that the indexical question comes first — and not consciousness-primacy. He states it flatly: consciousness and belief “do not play any fundamental role.” Qualia are “a different field of inquiry.” A footnote in the 2026 paper passionately opposes esoteric readings of his work, comparing his observer-dependence to length contraction in relativity: hard mathematical relations, indifferent to what anyone wishes or feels.

Take that seriously, because of what it does to this book. Müller is the strongest test we have of whether the pattern in these pages is real or wanted. He hands the inquiry its central shape — observers first, world downstream — and then declines every metaphysical prize on offer. A convergence that arrives from someone actively refusing the conclusion is worth more than ten that arrive from people hoping for it.

Now the debts, and they are real.

The proofs are for a model he says must be replaced. The theorems are genuine, but they hold inside the “bit model” of the 2020 paper, and Müller himself has moved on from it — the 2026 paper argues by what he openly calls plausibility arguments, offered as a guide for proofs in a formalism that does not yet exist. That is honest, and it is a promissory note. Do not read the theorems as covering the full programme.

The most quotable parts are the least supported. Subjective immortality, “falling out of the world,” what it means to switch off a simulated mind — these are the passages that travel, and they lean on a forgetting mechanism Müller admits his current model cannot represent and is still building. Speculative material sitting inside an already-cautious framework. Enjoy it; don’t bank on it.

And a live one he names himself: recovering full quantum theory from the programme is still open, and he treats it as the main test. Not passed yet.

Where it lands in this book: Müller is the one who removes something the others keep needing. Schooler puts a top on the nest to hold the self together; Kastrup starts from one great mind and asks where it divides. Both need a self that lasts long enough to be built or broken. Müller doesn’t have one to argue about — and he gets to a shared, lawful world anyway. That is not a settlement of their quarrel. It is a demonstration that the quarrel had a premise in it, which is the business of the last chapter of this book.
