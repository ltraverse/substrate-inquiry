The Scroll · One

The Authors of the Upanishads

The witness is the whole.

Three thousand years ago, in the forests of what is now India, people sat under trees and went hunting for the one thing you’d think would be easiest to find: themselves. Not the body, not the name — the one who looks out from behind your eyes. They never found it. And what they made of not finding it is one of the boldest ideas a human being has ever had. Their notes survived, memorized and whispered down the centuries, as the Upanishads. Start where they started — with something you can try right now.

Try to catch yourself looking

Look at your hand.

Easy. Now turn it around: look at the one looking at the hand. Catch the seer in the act.

Go on, try. What you’ll grab is a thought — a picture of “me,” a tightness somewhere in the chest, your own name arriving like a label. Never the looker itself. Whatever you catch has already flipped over into a thing looked-at, and the real seer has stepped back a pace, still quietly seeing. Reach again; it dodges again. It’s a little like trying to bite your own teeth.

The forest teachers wrote it in a line that still rings: you cannot see the seer of seeing. Not a paradox to solve — a plain report. The one looking is never out among the things it looks at. It’s what you’re looking with.

So how do you talk about something you can never catch?

You can’t say what it is. Call it a something and you’ve made it one more thing to look at, and it has slipped behind you again. So the teachers did the only thing left. They said what it isn’t.

Two words: neti neti — “not this, not this.”

Walk it yourself. Your body? You can watch it, feel it, meet it in a mirror — so it’s on the seen side. Not this. Your thoughts? You can watch one show up and leave. Not this. Your feelings, your face, the whole story of your life? Every one of them a thing you can turn and notice — so none of them is the noticer. Peel away everything you can observe about yourself, and the one thing left, the thing you can never peel because it’s doing the peeling, is bare awareness. They called it Ātman: not your personality, not your self-image, just the plain fact that you’re awake at all.

The light nobody sees

Here’s the part that stops you.

You never actually see light. You see the hand, the wall, this page — by means of light, which stays invisible while it shows you everything else. Awareness works the same way. You never catch it; you only ever know things by being aware of them. The most obvious thing there is, and the most hidden, for the very same reason: it’s what you see with, never what you see.

The teachers found it hiding in plain sight across your whole day. Awake: you, and a world. Dreaming: you, and a world your own mind is quietly building out of nothing. Deep, dreamless sleep: no world at all — and yet you wake and say “I slept well,” so something was there in the dark, present, with nothing to be present to. Three rooms, three completely different scenes, one thing steady through all of them — you, the awareness they happen in. Think of a screen the movies play on. The screen is never in any movie. No movie plays without it.

The claim that changed everything

Everything so far, you can check for yourself. Here the teachers do something you can’t — the leap that made them the Upanishads.

That bare awareness, they said, is not a little private thing rattling around in your skull. It isn’t yours at all. It’s the same awareness looking out of every pair of eyes — and, they pushed further, the same as the one reality underneath everything that exists. The awareness behind your face and the ground of the whole universe: a single thing.

Three words carry it: tat tvam asi — you are that. Not part of that. Not from that. Are.

Picture a wave. It stands up with its own shape and edge, looks like a separate thing for a moment — but there is no wave sitting on the water. The wave is the ocean, doing a wave. You are like that, they said: not a lonely spark of awareness marooned in a huge dead universe, but the one reality itself, looking out from the single spot it calls “me.” They even left a strange little sum for it — take the whole away from the whole, and the whole is still there. You are not a chip off the infinite. You are the whole of it, entire, looking out from here.

The nugget

You are not the things you can notice about yourself. You are the noticing — and the noticing, followed all the way down, is not small and not yours. It is the one awareness that everything shares.

To know that — not as an idea, but as the ground under your feet — was, for these teachers, the end of fear. If you are the whole, nothing outside you can take you away, because there is no outside. Which is why they left a warning that still lands like a struck bell: from death to death goes he who sees any difference here. Mistake the many separate things for the final truth, and you die with each of them, again and again.

Hold it honestly

This book holds everything one way: as if it were true, and worth living inside — never as proven. So split this page down the middle.

The first half you can test tonight. That you can never catch the seer; that awareness is the light you see by and never see. Sit for sixty seconds and look. Nearly three thousand years later a German named Kant walks up to that same wall by cold logic alone, half a world away — you’ll meet him further on.

The second half — that this one awareness is the ground of all being — is the leap, and its sharpest challenger is already waiting on the next page. The Buddha starts exactly here, at the self you can’t catch, and walks out the opposite door: no witness at all, only the witnessing. Hold both as you go. The argument between them is the whole book.

“You cannot see the seer of seeing, you cannot hear the hearer of hearing, you cannot think the thinker of thinking, you cannot know the knower of knowing.” — Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 3.4.2
