The Scroll · Two

The Buddha

Cognizing is real — but there is no one who cognizes.

Twenty-five centuries ago a man walked out of a palace to find out why living hurts. He is remembered as the Buddha — “the awake one” — and when the long search was over he sat down and did something plainer than any prayer: he watched his own experience, closely, the way you might watch water to learn how it moves. On the page before this, the forest teachers watched that same moving water and found one still witness underneath it — the Self, the ground of everything. The Buddha stood at the very same spot, looked as hard as a person can look, and came back with the opposite news. There is no one there. Only the looking.

How could a careful mind arrive at something that strange? Follow it.

Take yourself apart into what’s actually here

Sit for a second and ask a blunt question: what is my experience made of right now? Not in theory — now.

The Buddha tipped the whole of it out into five heaps. Khandhas, he called them — just “bundles.” Watch them turn up. There’s the body, the solid stuff, the chair pressing back against you. There’s feeling — not emotion, just the raw tone of each moment: nice, not-nice, or neither. There’s recognizing — the mind going “chair,” “red,” “her voice,” sorting the flood into knowns. There are the reactions — the wants and habits and flinches that stir in response. And there’s knowing itself, the bare fact that anything registers at all, the light the other four show up in.

Now look for the you that owns the five. Look for the extra thing — the little self standing back, having all this. Search honestly. You’ll turn up another feeling, another thought wearing the label “me”: one more item in the pile. You find the bundle. You never find the one holding it.

Look hardest at the knower — that’s where we hide

One of the five feels different. The body comes and goes, fine; feelings pass, fine. But surely the knowing — the one aware of all the rest — surely that’s me, the steady watcher behind the eyes?

A monk named Sāti thought so. He decided this knowing was the real self, one continuous thread riding on from life to life, unchanged. The Buddha called him in and corrected him harder than almost anywhere in the record — because Sāti had slipped the old idea of a soul in through the back door and pinned it on consciousness.

The correction is where it gets beautiful. Knowing is not one thing that stays. It doesn’t sit behind the senses, waiting. It flares up only at contact — when a sense meets something. Eye meets a shape: seeing happens. Ear meets a sound: hearing happens. The knowing doesn’t exist first and then reach out; it arises because the meeting arises, and it’s even named after it.

His picture is a fire. A fire has no nature of its own that carries between fuels — you name it by what it burns. Wood gives a wood-fire; grass gives a grass-fire; same word, but no single flame slips out of the wood and travels into the grass. Blow out the fuel and nothing flies off to wait somewhere else. Knowing is like that. Eye-and-shape makes seeing; ear-and-sound makes hearing; and there is no master-knower underneath, touring the senses with your name in its pocket. Just knowings, each lit by its own contact, each gone when the contact goes.

That is why Sāti was wrong. Nothing travels on, because nothing lasts even from one blink to the next.

Now test it yourself: is any of it yours to command?

The Buddha hands you a second test, plainer still, and you can run it where you sit.

Take each heap and ask: is this mine to rule? If the body were truly yours, your own, you could say “never fall ill,” and it would obey. It won’t. If feeling were yours, you could order “only the pleasant, from now on.” It doesn’t listen. If the knowing were yours, you could say “hold this one thought, exactly, and stay” — and keep it. Try. It’s gone in seconds; a new knowing has already taken the seat before you finished deciding to hold the last.

So the case closes quietly. Each heap arises and passes. What keeps dissolving can’t be leaned on, and that unease is dukkha. And a thing that won’t last and won’t take orders can’t honestly be called your self. That’s his word — anattā, not-self. Not “you have no experience.” Rather: nothing in the experience is the fixed, owning self you assumed was running it. The self was never one of the pieces. It was a reading laid over them.

Then what holds it together, with no one holding it?

Fair question. If there’s no owner and no thread, why doesn’t the whole thing fly apart? Why does this moment feel like it belongs to the same “me” as a moment ago?

His answer isn’t a thing. It’s a law. Nothing here stands on its own; each piece leans on the next. Four small words: when this is, that is. Everything shows up because its conditions have shown up — the way a shadow appears exactly when a light and an object do, needing no shadow-stuff of its own. For the two deepest partners — knowing on one side, the body-and-naming on the other — his image is two sheaves of reeds propped upright against each other. Neither stands alone. Each holds the other up. Pull one and both drop. So there is no first thing, no foundation-self underneath. Just a standing pattern of things leaning — steady enough to look like a someone, with no someone there.

And the feeling of “I”? He calls it the conceit I am — a fabrication the process keeps throwing up about itself, the way a whirlpool can look like a solid object when it’s only water passing through a shape. The “I am” is real as a feeling that arises. It’s false only as a report — it promises an owner the looking never finds.

The chariot: how a thing can be real and empty at once

One last step, or the whole idea sounds like a denial of the obvious. A later teacher made it plain with a cart.

Point at a chariot. Is it the wheels? No. The axle? The pole? The frame? No single part is the chariot. Now strip the parts away, one by one — is the chariot hiding among them, some chariot-stuff over and above them? It isn’t. And yet you’re not lying when you say “chariot.” The word is true. It names parts standing in the right relation, and points at nothing extra behind them.

That’s his “you,” exactly. “Leon,” “me,” “the self” — a true, useful name for five heaps running in lawful order, with no owner-substance behind the word. The name isn’t false. The thing it seems to promise — a separate someone, in there, having all this — is simply not there. Both at once. The whole view balances on that edge: the knowing is real; the knower is not.

The nugget

There is seeing, but no separate seer; there is knowing, but no knower behind it. What you call “I” is a true name for a bundle of happenings leaning on each other by law — not the owner of them, but one more thing the bundle keeps making. The experience is real. The one you assume is having it is a story the experience tells about itself.

He didn’t mean it as cleverness. He meant it as a way out. No fixed self means no fixed self to guard, to lose, to be wounded — and the grip that drives so much of our suffering, the endless clutch for me and mine, is left holding nothing.

Hold it honestly

Hold it as-if, not as-proven — and notice it opens straight onto a fight, not a truce.

The Buddha and the forest teachers began at the same discovery, the one you checked a page ago: you cannot catch the seer. Turn to look at the one looking, and you find only more things looked-at. Both saw it clearly. Both stood dead still in front of it.

Then they walked out opposite doors. The Upanishads: you can’t catch the witness because you are it — the one awareness under everything, ever-present, never an object. The Buddha: you can’t catch it because there isn’t one — pull the layers back and you don’t reach a silent watcher, you reach the end of watchers; there is only watching, arising and passing by conditions. One made the un-catchable the deepest Something. The other made it a genuine No-one.

Which is right, no one can settle from the outside — that’s the leap, and both of them take it. The sixty-second experiment shows only that the seer isn’t findable; it stays silent on whether that means everything or nothing. Two people looked through one doorway and saw opposite worlds. Every thinker still ahead in this book is, in some way, trying to stand in that doorway and see straight.

“Consciousness is reckoned by the very condition dependent upon which it arises. When it arises dependent on the eye and forms, it is reckoned as eye-consciousness… Just as a fire is reckoned by the particular condition dependent on which it burns — a log-fire, a grass-fire — so too consciousness is reckoned by its condition.” — Majjhima Nikāya 38, Mahātaṇhāsaṅkhaya Sutta
