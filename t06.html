The Scroll · Six

John Locke

You reach only as far as your consciousness reaches back.

John Locke was an English doctor and philosopher writing in the 1600s — a practical man who trusted plain observation over grand systems. Where Descartes, the page before, hunted for one thing he couldn’t doubt, Locke asks a question you could put to a jury: what makes the person standing here today the same person who did something years ago? It sounds obvious, until you push — and when you push, everything you’d reach for first falls away. It’s a quiet idea. It changed how we think about who we are, and it still runs under our courtrooms.

Start with a question you answer every day

“Are you the same person who broke that window when you were seven?” You say yes without thinking. But why yes? What stays the same across all those years, so the child and the grown-up count as one person and not two?

Try the obvious answers. Watch each crumble.

The same body? Can’t be. Your body isn’t one fixed thing; it’s a river. The cells swap out, the atoms you’re built from now were somewhere else a few years back, the seven-year-old’s body is physically gone. If being the same person meant being the same stuff, you’d be a stranger to your own childhood.

The same soul — some hidden spiritual substance underneath? Here Locke’s move is bold and honest: even if such soul-stuff exists, it can’t be what makes you you. Suppose that same soul had lived an earlier life you can’t remember a scrap of — some ancient person, gone from you completely. Would you answer for what they did? Of course not. You’d feel no more tied to them than to a stranger. So sameness of the underlying stuff — body or soul — isn’t what we mean by “the same person.” Something else is doing the work.

His answer: the thread is consciousness

Here’s the idea, and it’s startlingly simple. What makes you the same person over time is consciousness — by which he means “the perception of what passes in a man’s own mind.” Your awareness of your own thoughts and deeds. The power to turn back, find a past thought or act, and know it as yours.

You are the same person as far back as your consciousness can reach and take hold of a past moment — “and no further.” Three words doing enormous work. The self doesn’t stretch as far as your body, or your soul, or the calendar. It stretches exactly as far as consciousness can reach backward and lay claim to what it finds.

See what that makes the self. Not a lump of matter, not a spark of soul — a reach. A living thread of awareness running back through time, gathering up the moments it can still own. You are the length of that thread.

The prince and the cobbler

Locke liked to test an idea by imagining something wild, to see which way your own judgment jumps. So: one night the entire consciousness of a prince — every memory, his whole sense of himself, everything he can look back on and call mine — leaves his body and wakes up inside a cobbler, a poor shoemaker, while the cobbler’s own consciousness is gone. The prince’s body lies there, empty of that inner life.

Now — who is the person walking around in the cobbler’s body?

You feel the answer at once. The prince — same person, wrong body. He remembers the palace, owns the prince’s deeds, calls the prince’s life his own. The person went with the consciousness, not the body. And there’s the edge: if the person goes with the consciousness, the body is just clothing. The self isn’t the stuff you’re made of. It’s the awareness that reaches back and says “that was me.”

The self is not a thing — it’s an act

This is the heart of it, so slow down. We picture the self as a thing you have — a little core inside, sitting there, being you. Locke quietly swaps that for a doing.

The self, for him, is an act of appropriation. “Appropriation” just means claiming something as your own — like putting your name on a bag. Consciousness reaches out across your past, touches a thought here, a deed there, and folds them in: mine, mine, that too is mine. That gathering-in, that binding of scattered moments into one owner, is the self. There’s no owner sitting behind the act, waiting; the owning is the owner. You aren’t a thing that happens to remember. You are the remembering, doing its work of claiming.

Where it all comes from: the white paper

Step back and ask where the mind gets anything to be conscious of. Locke’s famous answer sits under the whole picture. At the start the mind is white paper — blank, nothing stamped in before experience. (He was writing against people who thought we’re born already knowing certain truths; he saw no such thing in a newborn.)

So how does the paper get written on? Two channels, only two. The first is sensation — the outer senses, sight and sound and touch carrying the world in and leaving their marks. The obvious one.

The second is quieter, and for our story the important one: reflection. An inner sense — the mind watching its own workings. Not seeing the world, but catching itself seeing; noticing itself think, doubt, want, remember. Reflection is the mind turned back on its own activity. And that turning-back is the very material a self is made of, because the self is consciousness owning its own past acts. The inner sense that watches the mind is the same power by which the mind claims itself across time.

Why “person” is a courtroom word

One last piece, and it locks the whole thing shut. Locke says flatly that “person” is a forensic term — a word built for the courtroom, for handing out praise and blame, reward and punishment.

Sit with why that matters. If we only ever punish and reward persons, and a person is exactly what consciousness can reach and own, then we can only justly hold you to account for what you can actually own as yours. That’s not a soft idea. It’s a hard, practical one, and it ties the whole self to a real limit: the reach of your own consciousness. What you can’t reach back to and claim, you are not — and so, in the deepest sense, it isn’t yours to answer for. Locke built the self out of exactly the stuff a fair judgment would need it built from.

The nugget

You are not the body you’re made of, or some hidden soul-stuff underneath. You are a reach of consciousness — a living thread of awareness that stretches back through time, gathering up the moments it can still own and binding them into one owner. You are as far back as you can reach and say “that was me,” and no further.

To see this is to see the self as something done, not something had — and to feel, gently, where its edges are.

Hold it honestly

Hold it as-if, not as-proven — and look first at where Locke and the man before him split, because the split is the whole lesson.

Descartes made the self a substance — a thinking thing that simply exists, whole and continuous, whether or not it remembers a moment of its past. For him, you are; the self is a solid fact (and it took God to hold it steady across time). Locke flatly refuses. He lifts the self off any substance and sets it on a relation — the reach of memory-consciousness. A real break. For Descartes the self is a thing that has thoughts; for Locke the self just is the thread of owned thoughts, nothing more solid underneath. Where Descartes leaned on God to bind the self, Locke lets it be bound — or broken — by consciousness alone.

And here’s the unsettling place that leads, which you can weigh against your own sense of fairness. If the self reaches only as far as consciousness reaches, then cut the reach — forget completely — and the deed drops out of the self. The part of your life you can no longer reach back to and own is, for Locke, strictly not you. Profound insight, or real problem — philosophers have fought over it ever since, because it seems to say a man who has genuinely, totally forgotten a crime is not the same person who did it, which no court accepts. Locke saw the sting and half-embraced it. The daring of the idea and its exposed nerve, in one stroke.

What’s solidly checkable: the plain fact he starts from — your body swaps out its matter entirely, so bodily stuff can’t be what your sameness rests on. And reflection you can feel in a second: turn your attention onto your own thinking and catch the mind watching itself. That inner sense is real. The leap is the claim that consciousness is all there is to being the same person — that with the memory gone, the person is gone. That you can’t prove from outside; you can only weigh it against how it feels to lose a memory, and against what justice seems to need. One self, built by two careful men from opposite materials — a substance, and a reach. Their disagreement is the shape of how hard it is to say what you are.

“…person… is a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places; which it does only by that consciousness which is inseparable from thinking… As far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any past action or thought, so far reaches the identity of that person.” — John Locke, An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, II.xxvii.9
